Part 0The problem with the obvious approach

One factor at a time is not caution. It is a bias.

Holding everything else fixed feels rigorous. It quietly guarantees that you cannot see the one thing most likely to matter.

Three separate failures

  • It is less efficient. Every OFAT run informs exactly one effect. Every factorial run informs all of them, because the design is orthogonal.
  • It cannot see interactions. If the best pull-back angle depends on the stop angle, OFAT will report whichever answer the fixed level happened to favour.
  • It hides its own failure. The output looks clean. Nothing in it flags the corner you never visited.

On this machine

Exercise 2 will measure the pull-back × stop-angle interaction at 2.88 m — the second-largest term in the entire model, larger than three of the five main effects. An OFAT study of this catapult does not merely estimate it poorly. It never asks.

Take away

OFAT is not a conservative version of DOE. It is a different experiment that answers a narrower question and does not tell you so.

Lecture notes

The efficiency argument is the one everybody teaches, and it is the weakest of the three. Doubling your run count is a budget problem. Missing a 2.88 m interaction is a wrong answer, and the report will not carry any sign that it is wrong.

Draw the two-factor picture on the board if the slide is not enough. The OFAT explorer walks along the two axes from a base point and never occupies the far corner. If the response surface has a ridge running diagonally, the OFAT path walks parallel to it and concludes the process is flat.

Ask: "Who has run an OFAT study at work?" Almost every hand goes up. Follow with: "Did anything in the output tell you whether an interaction was present?" It cannot — that is the point.

Forward reference to keep them honest: promise that in Exercise 2 you will show them the actual number, from this machine, and that it will beat most of the main effects. Then deliver it on the alias reconciliation slide.

Part 0The strategy behind the three exercises

Never spend the whole budget on the first experiment

Experimentation is sequential. Each stage answers a different question and hands the next one a smaller, better-aimed problem. Box's guidance: commit no more than a quarter of your resources to the opening move.

Stage 1 · Screen

Which factors matter at all?

Many factors, two levels, deliberately few runs. Accepts confounding as the price of cheapness.

Exercise 1 · 25−2 (8 runs) and Plackett–Burman (12 runs) · 20 shots

Stage 2 · Characterise

How do the survivors act together?

Fewer factors, all combinations. Buys clean interaction estimates and a curvature check.

Exercise 2 · 25 · 32 runs, then + 4 centre · 36 shots

Stage 3 · Optimise

Where exactly is the setting I want?

Three levels, quadratic model, a target rather than a maximum — and variation as a second response.

Exercise 3 · Box-Behnken · 15 runs · 15 shots

Why not just run Exercise 3 first?

A five-factor Box-Behnken is 46 runs. Even fired once each that is 46 shots to map a space in which two of the five factors turn out not to matter — and it would still tell you nothing about variation.

Take away

You are not running one experiment. You are running three, and the first one's job is to be cheap and slightly wrong.

Lecture notes

The line worth repeating until it sticks: the first experiment's job is to be cheap and slightly wrong. Students trained on textbook problems expect every experiment to produce a defensible final answer. A screening design does not, and is not supposed to.

Box's 25% rule is a heuristic, not a theorem, but it has the right shape: you do not know enough at the start to spend well, so buy information about where to spend before spending. Note that our course happens to land close to it — 8 shots of an eventual 71 is 11%, and Exercises 1 and 2 together are 79%. The unreplicated course is much more front-loaded, because the expensive back end is the part that needed the replicates.

Ask them to predict now, before any data: which two of the five factors will turn out not to matter? Write the guesses down. Pull-back angle is the obvious guess for "matters most" and is correct; the group usually splits on pin elevation versus arm hole, and pin elevation is the one that dies.

The 690-shot arithmetic in the right-hand panel is worth doing on the board slowly. It is the most concrete justification for sequential experimentation that the course contains.

Part 0Why fractionating works at all

Two empirical facts that the whole method rests on

Cutting an experiment down is only safe because real processes are built a particular way. These are observations about the physical world, not mathematical results — and they can fail.

Effect sparsity

Of the many effects you could estimate, only a few are large. Box and Meyer's Pareto reading: roughly 20% of the factors account for around 80% of the variation.

On this machine: a 25 design contains 31 estimable effects. Exercise 2 will find that five of them carry 95% of the sum of squares, and the remaining 26 share what is left.

This is why an 8-run design can rank five factors. It is betting that the big terms are few, and on real processes that bet usually pays.

Effect heredity

An interaction is likely to matter only if at least one of its parent main effects matters. Large interactions between two negligible factors are rare.

On this machine: the three largest interactions are AB, AD and AC — every one of them contains A, the largest main effect. The interactions among the weak factors (CD, CE, DE) sit at 0.11%, 0.00% and 0.00%.

Heredity is what lets you drop a factor after screening and not worry that you have discarded a hidden interaction with it.

Take away

Fractional designs are not free. They are cheap because processes are sparse and hereditary — assumptions you inherit, should state, and can check later.

Lecture notes

Emphasise that these are empirical regularities, not laws. A student who says "but what if my process isn't sparse?" is asking the right question. The answer: then a fractional design will mislead you, and the way you find out is the confirmation run at the end.

Both numbers on this slide are real and come from Exercise 2's fit, so you can promise the class they will verify them personally in about ninety minutes. Making the framing checkable is what stops this slide feeling like received wisdom.

Heredity has weak and strong forms. Weak: an interaction needs at least one significant parent. Strong: it needs both. This machine satisfies weak heredity plainly — AB, AC and AD all inherit from A — but AB also has a strongly significant B, so it satisfies the strong form too. You do not need the terminology unless the group is advanced.

Instructor only — the sparsity number is computable from the effects table on the Exercise 2 results slide: A 64.77 + AB 9.75 + B 8.92 + D 6.48 + C 5.05 = 95.0% of total SS across five terms.

Lecture notes

Do not read this slide aloud line by line. Put it up, give them ninety seconds, then drill the two highlighted rows only.

Effect versus coefficient. Going from −1 to +1 is a change of 2 coded units, so the effect is 2b. Every piece of software reports one or the other and they rarely tell you which. Make the class state which one their tool gives before they compare anything to the answer key.

Percent contribution versus p. Plant this now because Exercise 2 will make it urgent: with 32 shots, terms contributing 0.27% come out statistically significant. Significance answers "is it real?"; contribution answers "do I care?". Students who have only ever been taught p < 0.05 find this genuinely destabilising, which is the point.

Quick check before moving on: "A coded model gives b = 1.44 for AB. What is the AB effect, and what does it mean physically?" Answer: 2.88 m, and it means A's effect is 2.88 m bigger at B high than at B low.

Part 1The five inputs

What you can set

Five factors. Two are angles, three are discrete positions on the machine. Each has a distinct physical route to the response, which is what makes the interactions real rather than decorative.

FactorRangePhysical route to distance
APull-back angle90 – 200° How far the arm is drawn. Sets band elongation, so it sets stored energy. 90° is straight up, 180° horizontal, beyond 180° below horizontal.
BStop angle90 – 120° Where the arm meets the stop and the ball leaves the cup. Sets the launch angle, and therefore how energy is split between height and range.
CBungee position1.0 – 5.0 Band stiffness, 88 N/m to 200 N/m. More energy stored per unit of stretch.
DArm hole1.0 – 5.0 Cup position along the arm, 0.6 m to 1.0 m. Raises release radius — but also rotational inertia. Two competing mechanisms in one dial.
EPin elevation1.0 – 5.0 Raises the stop pin, adding 1.5° per unit to the effective stop angle. A fine trim on B.

Read the table for interactions before you run anything

A sets energy. B decides how that energy is aimed. Those cannot be independent — more energy is worth more when it is aimed well. AB is predictable from the physics, and Exercise 2 will confirm it. Encourage this reasoning; a designed experiment should test mechanisms you have already thought about.

The one that fights itself

D raises release speed through v = ωr and lowers it through I = ⅓ML² + mr². Ask which wins: the rod term is 0.083 against the ball's 0.016–0.045, so leverage wins and D stays monotonic — damped, not reversed.

Coded windows

The screening exercises and the response-surface exercise use different low/high values for A, B and C. Mixing them up silently produces wrong coded values.

Lecture notes

Spend real time on D. It is the only factor with two opposing mechanisms, and it is the factor that behaves most strangely in the results. Students who understand why before they see the data will read the screening result far better.

Then set the trap deliberately. The mechanism is real but mild — over its own window D moves the ball about 2.3 m with almost no curvature, much like B and C. So when Exercise 1 reports 5.19 m for D, the physical story is sitting right there to explain it, and it is the wrong explanation: 2.88 m of that column is the AB interaction. A plausible mechanism makes a biased estimate more convincing, not less, which is the single best reason to know your alias structure before you interpret anything.

Push the class to predict signs before running anything. Pull-back up, distance up — obvious. Stop angle up means a more lofted shot, which for this geometry increases distance over the window used here. Bungee stiffer, distance up. All three are correct, and getting them right builds the confidence needed to be suspicious later when the screening design reports something that contradicts the physics.

The two-window warning at the bottom is error number nine on the instructor guide's list of common failures. Flag it here and again at the start of Exercise 3. The full factor table appears in the workbook's Appendix A.

Ask: "Which factor do you expect to interact with which?" Steer toward A×B. Then tell them you will hold them to it — the answer arrives in Exercise 2 and it is 2.88 m.

Part 1Two responsesand only one of them is printed for you

What you measure

Distance is handed to you. Variation is not — you have to buy it with replicates. That asymmetry shapes every design decision in the course.

Y₁ · Distance from the pivot (m)

Ground impact measured along the arena ruler, with the pivot at 0 m.

Note the cup sits behind the pivot at release whenever the effective stop angle exceeds 90° — up to about half a metre. The reported number accounts for that, so it always matches the landing flag on the ruler.

Y₂ · Variation (m)

The standard deviation of repeated shots at one setting. Not printed. You measure it — with replicates.

This revision fires one shot per setting, so s is never computable and Y₂ does not exist anywhere in this course. Everything downstream of it goes too. Watch for it as we go; it disappears quietly.

The trap in the results table

The app prints a column called Predicted σ. It is a model output — a first-order error-propagation estimate — not a measurement. Using it as your variation response means fitting a model to a model. Students do this every single cohort. Say the words "predicted sigma is not data" out loud.

Why the asymmetry matters

A mean over n shots has standard error σ/√n. A standard deviation is a far noisier statistic at the same n. That single fact is why the replicated course runs 3 replicates in Exercises 1 and 2 and 15 in Exercise 3 — and why 1 costs the second response entirely while barely touching the first.

Take away

Two responses on paper, one measurable here. The gap between them is where robust engineering lives, and this revision cannot reach it — which is the destination of this course.

Lecture notes

The "Predicted σ" trap is worth pre-empting hard. It is a genuinely useful column — it lets an instructor check a student's answer instantly — but a student who regresses on it has skipped the entire reason replication exists and will get a suspiciously clean variation model.

Foreshadow the destination honestly: tell them the mean model and the variation model have different dominant factors, that this is what makes it possible to hold the mean on target while cutting the spread — and that this revision will not be able to show them the second model at all. Asking them to watch for the moment it goes missing keeps Exercise 3 from feeling like an anticlimax.

Instructor only — in the replicated course the mean is led by A at 38.4% and variation by C at 42.7%. Hold it back until the Exercise 3 debrief, where you will have to supply it rather than derive it.

Part 1Where the numbers come from

The physics is solved, not faked

Each shot runs a real mechanics chain. That is why the response surface is genuinely curved and the interactions are genuinely there — nobody wrote them into a formula.

1 · elastic energy released between pull-back and stop E = ½·k·( Δx²pull − Δx²stop ) Δx = stretch past rest length 2 · rotational inertia of arm plus ball I = ⅓·Marm·L² + m·r²cup 3 · release ω = √( 2E / I ) v₀ = ω·rcup 4 · ballistic flight, α = θeff − 90° vx = v₀cos α / ( 1 + 0.02·v₀ ) vy = v₀sin α ← drag in vx only tflight = ( vy + √( vy² + 2g·ycup ) ) / g distance = xcup + vx·tflight xcup ≤ 0 (cup behind pivot)

One constants file feeds both the solver and the renderer, so the drawing cannot drift from the maths.

Where the curvature comes from

Step 4 is trigonometric. Horizontal speed carries cos α, flight time carries sin α, and distance is their product — so launch angle has a real optimum. On this machine it sits near 33°, past the top of B's window, so across the window B climbs with a visibly falling slope while A and C stay close to straight. That deceleration is the B² term Exercise 3 will fit.

The animation is the physics

The arc you watch is sampled from the same solved trajectory that produced the recorded number — not a decorative parabola drawn alongside it. A shot knocked off course by noise still flies a true parabola to its own landing mark.

Take away

Nothing in this simulator was reverse-engineered from a desired answer. The interactions and the curvature are consequences of the mechanics.

Lecture notes

You do not need to derive any of this. The point of the slide is credibility: students who suspect the answer was planted stop treating the analysis as discovery. Show the chain, note that it is four ordinary steps of first-year mechanics, and move on.

The one line worth dwelling on is step 4's trigonometry, because it is the physical origin of the quadratic term students will fit in Exercise 3. When B² comes out as the largest squared term, this is why. Point at it now so the callback lands later. Be precise about the optimum: it is real but it lies outside the window we run, so nobody will see distance actually fall. What they will see is the slope flattening, which is what a negative squared term looks like from inside the window.

Worth one sentence, because it pre-explains Exercise 2's headline: the chain is multiplicative. Energy set by A multiplies launch geometry set by B. Two factors that multiply cannot be described by a model that only adds them, so an AB interaction of several metres is a consequence of the mechanics rather than a surprise in the data.

If someone asks why drag lives inside vx rather than being subtracted from the finished distance: so that the drawn arc and the reported number are the same trajectory. Applying it afterwards would make the picture lie by a couple of percent.

Part 1Variation, and why it is not a formula

The machine has slop in it

Every shot perturbs the machine's physical state and re-solves the mechanics. Scatter in distance is therefore emergent: it depends on the settings, instead of following an assumed error term.

Source of variationMagnitude (1σ)
Band stiffness drift2.0% relative
Stop-angle rebound0.28°
Cup radius play0.004 m
Operator pull-back error0.60°
Ball mass1.5% relative
Tape measurement0.040 m

Five of the six are perturbations of the machine. Only the last is measurement error, and it is the smallest contributor at most settings.

The consequence

Because the noise enters before the mechanics rather than after, σ is a function of where you are in the factor space. Two settings that land in the same place on average can scatter by very different amounts.

You will verify this in ten minutes

Lab 0 fires 20 shots at two settings. The standard deviation at one is 0.22 m; at the other, 0.36 m. Same machine, same day, nothing changed but the dials.

Take away

Constant variance is an assumption, not a fact. On this machine it is false, and the course is built on that being false.

Lecture notes

The architectural point deserves a sentence: most teaching simulators add a fixed random number to a formula, which makes σ constant by construction and makes robust optimisation impossible. This one perturbs the physical state and re-solves, so heteroscedasticity is emergent.

Instructor only — hold this back until the Exercise 3 debrief. The stop-angle rebound term is multiplied by (1 + 0.055 × release velocity): the arm bounces off the stop less repeatably the harder it arrives. That single coupling is the entire mechanism behind the robustness lab. If you reveal it now, Exercise 3's best discussion evaporates, because students will simply read the answer off the slide instead of inferring it from a fitted model.

If a student works it out early from the table, congratulate them privately and ask them not to announce it. That happens roughly one cohort in four.

Part 1Two controls that make the course teachable

Seed and Noise

One makes results reproducible across thirty laptops. The other lets you change how badly the machine misbehaves without touching the physics.

Seed · reproducibility as a contract

Enter a seed and the generator restarts at the top of every batch. Same design, same replicates, same seed, same noise level ⇒ the same data, shot for shot — in the browser and in the offline toolkit alike.

That is what lets these slides quote exact numbers and lets students check themselves against course/data/.

The catch: editing the configuration table by hand instead of using Load DOE design changes the run order, and therefore the random draws. Conclusions still match; third decimals do not.

Noise · the machine's condition today

0×Perfectly repeatable. Every shot identical.
0.5×A well-behaved process.
1×As specified. Use for every exercise here.
2–3×Poor condition. Small effects start disappearing.
5×Barely usable without heavy replication.

It is not a sixth factor. It is how well maintained the machine is — a property of the process, not a setting you optimise.

Watch for

A student whose results are uniformly wider or narrower than everyone else's has almost certainly left the Noise control off 1×. Check this before debugging anything else.

Lecture notes

Two 30-second demonstrations, worth doing live rather than describing. First: fire ten shots at 0× and ten at 3× on the same setting. Nothing about the physics changed; the spread is unrecognisable. That establishes what "process variation" means physically before any statistics are attempted.

Second: run any design twice with the same seed and show the identical numbers, then clear the seed and show them move. Students trust a simulator they have seen behave deterministically on demand.

Note for the curious: the noise control scales every magnitude in the table on the previous slide, but deliberately does not scale the velocity coupling, because that term sets the shape of the variance structure rather than its size. Scaling it too would flatten the lofted-versus-flat difference and destroy the robustness exercise.

Ask why 0× is a legitimate teaching state rather than a bug. Answer: it makes pure error exactly zero, every F ratio infinite and every p-value 0 — a clean demonstration that significance testing is a comparison of signal against noise, and is meaningless without noise.

Vocabulary check: generator is the equation D = AB. Defining word is what you get by multiplying both sides by D, giving ABD = I. Students conflate the two constantly.

Why these particular generators? D = AB and E = AC is the standard minimum-aberration choice for a 25−2. Any pair of three-letter generators gives resolution III; this pair minimises how many short words appear in the defining relation.

Where it wins

At eight or more factors. Below that the regular design is already cheaper, which is why our five-factor catapult is a slightly artificial case — we run it here to compare the two on the same process, not because it saves shots.

What stays the same

Columns are balanced and orthogonal, exactly as in a regular design. Every main effect is still estimated independently of every other main effect. It is still a resolution III design.

What changes

The shape of the confounding. A regular design is regular: each main effect is fully tangled with a few specific interactions and perfectly clean against the rest. Plackett–Burman is non-regular, and the next two slides are about what that means.

Take away

Same resolution, different granularity of cost — and a completely different failure mode.

Lecture notes

Lead with the run-count ladder because it is the honest reason Plackett–Burman exists. Regular fractional factorials jump 8 → 16 → 32. If you have nine factors and can afford twelve runs, the regular family has nothing to sell you. Plackett and Burman's 1946 paper filled exactly those gaps.

Be upfront that five factors is not where a PB design pays. A 25−2 is 8 runs and a PB is 12. We are running it on this machine because comparing two screening designs against the same known truth is worth far more pedagogically than saving four shots — and because in Exercise 2 we will have the truth to score them both against.

Worth knowing: for N = 8, 16 and 32 the Plackett–Burman construction reproduces a regular fractional factorial. The design is only genuinely new when N is a multiple of four but not a power of two — 12, 20, 24, 28. The simulator will build any of those sizes, so you can demonstrate the equivalence at N = 8 if someone asks.

Exercise 1bThe defining difference

Concentrated bias, or bias spread thin

Both designs are resolution III. Neither can separate main effects from two-factor interactions. But they fail in opposite ways, and the pictures are not alike.

not aliased · r = 0 partially aliased · |r| = ⅓ fully confounded · |r| = 1 effect is part of the pair

Regular · the sniper

Six pairs are hopeless; every other pair is exactly clean. If the one interaction aliased onto your factor is large, that estimate is ruined and nothing hints at it. If it is small, the estimate is perfect. You can name the risk in advance from the generators alone.

Plackett–Burman · the fog

No estimate is ruined, and none is clean. Each carries a third of six different interactions — which partially cancel when their signs differ. You cannot name which term hurt you, and you need the design matrix, not the generators, to see the structure at all.

Lecture notes

The two pictures are the slide; let them sit for a moment before saying anything. The regular design is sparse and black-and-white. The Plackett–Burman is a uniform grey field. That visual difference is the regular/non-regular distinction, and students remember pictures long after they forget the word.

Make the trade-off explicit rather than picking a winner. Concentrated bias is predictable and catastrophic; diluted bias is unpredictable and survivable. Which you prefer depends on something you do not know yet — whether the interactions in your process are few and large, or many and moderate.

Note the partial cancellation, because it is the genuinely surprising property. Six terms at plus or minus a third do not add up to twice the damage of one term at full weight; with mixed signs they substantially cancel. We will see exactly that happen on the catapult in two slides — the PB estimate of D is off by 1.00 m where the regular design was off by 2.76 m.

Instructor only — the one-third figure is specific to N = 12. For a 20-run Plackett–Burman the correlations are ±1/5, for 24-run ±1/3 and ±1/6 in a mixed pattern. Larger designs dilute the bias further. If asked, the general statement is that non-regular designs spread the aliasing across many terms at fractional weights, and the weights depend on the specific design.

Exercise 1Debrief

What a resolution III design can and cannot do

True of both screens you ran — the regular 25−2 and the Plackett–Burman. Resolution III is a promise about what the design cannot separate, and neither flavour escapes it.

It genuinely delivers

  • A ranking. The ordering of the big factors is broadly right, and A really is dominant.
  • A drop decision. Something contributing 2.5% is a defensible candidate to fix and stop studying.
  • A pure-error estimate, from replication, independent of the model.
  • Enormous efficiency. Five factors triaged in 8 shots — a quarter of what the next exercise costs.

It structurally cannot deliver

  • Any main effect free of interaction. Every one carries a passenger. This is a property of the design, not of the data.
  • Any warning that it happened. Aliasing does not inflate p-values or dent R². The output looks identical either way.
  • An interaction estimate. Saturated: there is nowhere to put one.
  • A curvature check. No centre points, and two levels cannot see a bend.
The honest summary

A screening design tells you where to look next. It does not tell you what you found — and it will not say so unless you already know. Choosing between a regular fraction and a Plackett–Burman changes how it misleads you, not whether.

Lecture notes

The load-bearing sentence: aliasing does not degrade any diagnostic you would normally check. R² stays high. p-values stay tiny. Residuals look fine. There is no plot you can draw and no statistic you can compute from this data set that reveals the problem. The only protection is having derived the alias structure in advance.

This is why "trusting a resolution III design" is the most common conceptual failure in the whole course. It is not that students do not know what aliasing is — most can recite the definition. It is that the output is so reassuring that the definition stops feeling relevant.

Transition line into Exercise 2: "We have a ranking and no idea how much of it is real. The next design costs four times as much and buys exactly one thing — the ability to tell."

Exercise 2aWhy pay four times as much

32 runs, and every column means one thing

What the extra 24 runs buy

Exactly one thing, and it is not precision: the ability to separate main effects from two-factor interactions. Exercise 1 could not do it at any replicate count, because the limitation was structural rather than statistical.

The columns available

A 2⁵ design contains 31 estimable effects: 5 main, 10 two-factor, 10 three-factor, 5 four-factor and 1 five-factor. Nothing is confounded with anything. We will model the first 15 and let the rest fall into the residual — a decision with consequences we will inspect later.

Runs versus replicates

Going 8 → 32 runs is buying resolution. Going 1 → 3 replicates would be buying power. Two different purchases, and only one of them fixes aliasing.

Take away

Adding replicates to a resolution III design makes a confounded estimate more precise. It does not make it less confounded.

Lecture notes

The distinction in the last panel is worth the whole slide, and it is the sentence students most often get backwards on the exam: runs buy resolution, replicates buy power. Runs let you separate effects from each other; replicates let you separate effects from noise.

Make the failure mode concrete: if a team came back from Exercise 1 and said "we are not confident about D, let us run twenty replicates", they would obtain a beautifully precise estimate of D + AB. Precision on a biased quantity is worse than imprecision on an unbiased one, because it removes the wide confidence interval that might have made them cautious.

Exercise 2aInterpreting the interaction

What AB actually means at the machine

An interaction is not a statistical artefact to be reported. It is a mechanical statement: pull-back is worth more when the ball is aimed higher.

The mechanism

A sets how much energy is stored. B sets the angle it is fired at. A high stop angle lofts the shot, which converts speed into range efficiently — but only if there is speed to convert. At low pull-back there is almost nothing to aim, so aiming it well changes little.

Why "main effect of B" is a meaningless phrase here

B's effect is −0.07 m at A low and +5.30 m at A high. The reported main effect of 2.619 m is the average of two numbers, neither of which occurs anywhere on the machine. When an interaction is present, main effects are averages over conditions you never operate in.

Reading interaction plots

Parallel lines: no interaction. Diverging: the effect grows. Crossing: the best setting of one factor changes with the other — the case that ruins OFAT completely.

Lecture notes

Insist on physical explanations, not statistical ones. "AB is significant with p < 0.001" is not an interpretation. "Pull-back is worth more when the ball is lofted, because there is more speed available to convert into range" is.

The middle panel deserves the most attention and gets the least in most courses. A main effect in the presence of an interaction is an average over levels of the other factor. Here it averages −0.07 and 5.30 to get 2.619 — a number that describes no achievable configuration of the machine. It is not wrong, but it answers a question nobody asked.

Connect back to Exercise 1's alias table. This 2.69 m interaction is the quantity that inflated the D column. It was always there, always this large; the screening design simply had no column of its own to put it in.

Ask what would have happened if AB had been negative and roughly equal in size to D. The D column would have reported approximately zero, and the team would have dropped arm hole as irrelevant. Aliasing can hide a real factor as easily as it can invent one.

Exercise 3Choosing the design

Box-Behnken, and why not a central composite

Why Box-Behnken here

  • It never sets two factors to their extremes at once. No corner runs, so no simultaneously maximum pull-back and maximum stop angle.
  • Three levels, not five. Easier on a machine with discrete positions.
  • It stays inside the declared window. A CCD's axial points sit beyond it — at pull-back angles this machine may not reach.

When you would choose the CCD instead

When corners are safe and cheap, when you want to build on an existing factorial (a CCD is a factorial with axial runs bolted on, so it can be run sequentially), or when you need the rotatability its axial spacing provides.

Lecture notes

The sequential-build point is the strongest argument for the CCD and deserves a mention even though we are not using it: you can run a two-level factorial, test for curvature, and if you find it, add axial and centre runs to the design you already have. Box-Behnken cannot be grown that way — it is a design you commit to up front.

The corner-avoidance argument is not merely theoretical on physical equipment. On a real catapult, maximum pull-back combined with maximum band tension is exactly the setting that snaps something. Every experienced engineer in the room will recognise the constraint.

Run counts for reference, in case someone asks how this scales: Box-Behnken is 15 runs for 3 factors, 27 for 4, 46 for 5. It is not available for 2 factors at all — with two factors, use a CCD or a 3² factorial.

What did not move

C's p-value reads 0.0858, 0.0857, 0.0855, 0.0862 as the machine gets ten times noisier. In the replicated course the same column travelled nine orders of magnitude. Here it is flat to three decimal places.

So what is a p-value?

A statement about the evidence, not the effect. Effect size is a property of the process; the p-value is a property of the process and your experiment — including, here, its aliasing.

Why it stopped listening

At one shot per run the denominator is not noise — it is the interactions this resolution III design cannot separate. That bias is fixed. It does not care how hard the machine shakes, and it is what every F ratio divides by.

Take away

If a p-value does not move when the process gets ten times noisier, ask what it is a p-value for. This one is measuring the design's own bias, and reporting it in the notation of evidence.

Lecture notes

Power is the concept students most reliably fail to absorb from a lecture, which is why this is built as something they run rather than something they are told.

The classroom version: have the room run the 8-run design at 1× noise with eight different seeds and record whether C comes out significant. It comes out roughly one time in fourteen. Then have them add one replicate — 16 shots instead of 8 — and watch it come out every single time. That contrast is the lab.

Now the perverse row, which rewards a class that is paying attention. C is detected 7% of the time at 1× noise and 29% of the time at 3×. More noise, more detections. Once the denominator is bias rather than noise, adding noise does not reduce power — it just adds lottery tickets, and some of them win. Any student who can explain that row understands what a p-value is.

Then give them the full factorial at one shot per run for contrast: 32 shots, 16 residual df, and E detected 18% of the time at 1× noise. More runs bought the degrees of freedom; they did not buy the power.

The sentence to extract: runs buy resolution, replicates buy power. Runs separate effects from each other; replicates separate effects from noise. This entire revision is the second half of that sentence, written out at length.

The design that looks best is the one to fear

Read the two rows carefully

The good design got within 0.13 m of the truth and can say nothing about it — 16 runs, 16 parameters, no residual. The bad design overstated E more than fourfold, because E is aliased with AC and AC is genuinely large. Both fail. Only one of them makes its failure visible.

Why this is worse than low power

Low power gives a wide interval — a visible "be careful". Bias gives a narrow interval around the wrong number. More power for a biased estimate is worse than less power for an unbiased one.

A saturated design has three ways out

Pool terms into the residual2–5 of 15 found
Lenth's pseudo standard error1 of 15
Centre points as pure error13 of 15

The first two build an error term out of discarded signal. Only the third is made of noise — and it costs four runs already in the design.

Say this to the manager who wants to halve the experiment

Halving an experiment does not halve what you learn. It decides which questions you may no longer ask — and will not warn you which.

Lecture notes

Present the top table without the truth row and ask which design they would rather have used. Most rooms pick the 8-run design, because it is the only one that produced a number they recognise. Then reveal that E's true effect is 0.367 m: the design that could say nothing was within 0.13 m of it, and the design that spoke confidently was out by a factor of four.

Connect it straight back to the alias reconciliation slide in Exercise 2. This is the identical mechanism, identical alias pair (E with AC), simply observed at higher noise. What changed is not the statistics but the framing: earlier it was a curiosity about a screening design, and here it is a design-selection decision someone could plausibly get wrong at work.

Note the deeper point about statistics education: every diagnostic students are taught to check — p, R², confidence-interval width — measures precision. None of them measures bias. Bias is controlled at the design stage or not at all, which is why the alias table has to be derived before the data exists.

Ask for the one-sentence version they would say to a manager. The panel gives one; better ones come from the room. Grade on whether they distinguish precision from bias.

Part 5Marking and self-check

The errors that actually happen

#Error
1Trusting a resolution III design. The most common conceptual failure. Insist on the alias table before the analysis.
2Reading the effect off the coefficient. In coded units the effect is twice the coefficient.
3Treating a residual as pure error. With one shot per run they are not the same thing, and nothing in the output distinguishes them.
4Including centre points in the interaction fit. They carry no interaction information — reserve them for the curvature test.
5Declaring victory on R². R² of 0.99 coexists happily with a model you have no way to test.
6Chasing p-values. Here almost nothing is significant, and the estimates are fine anyway. Redirect to percent contribution.
#Error
7Reporting a boundary optimum as an interior one. C = −1 means "go softer than we tested", not "1.50 is optimal".
8Skipping confirmation. A model is a hypothesis until tested at a setting it has never seen.
9Mixing up the two factor windows. Screening and RSM use different low/high values for A, B and C.
10Regressing on "Predicted σ". It is a model output. Fit variation to measured standard deviations only.
11Treating noise as a sixth factor. It is a property of the machine, not a setting you optimise.
12Concluding a factor is absent because p > 0.05. Failing to detect is not demonstrating absence.
Marking note

Award full marks to a student whose confirmation run missed and who diagnosed why. Deduct from any report whose confirmation matches the prediction suspiciously exactly.

Lecture notes

Hand this list out at the start of the day rather than the end. It reads as a checklist, and students who have it in front of them during the labs make roughly half as many of these mistakes.

Errors 1 and 12 are flagged because they are conceptual rather than procedural. The other ten are things people do wrong and then fix. These two are things people believe, and belief survives being corrected once.

The marking note is worth saying out loud to the class before they write their reports. It changes the incentive from "produce a clean-looking result" to "produce an honest one", and the reports get markedly better.

Part 5Closing

Four things worth keeping

01 · Estimates are cheap. Evidence is not.

Eight shots ranked five factors correctly, within a few percent of the 24-shot answer — and could not establish that three of them were real.

02 · Resolution has a price, and it is paid up front

Aliasing is exact, predictable and computable before any data exists. Every diagnostic you were taught to check measures precision; none of them measures bias. Bias is a design decision.

03 · A target does not determine a unique answer

One equation, three unknowns, a ridge of 397 correct solutions — an opportunity only if you have a second response to spend it on.

04 · What replication actually buys

Not a better number. It buys pure error, and with it every significance test, every adequacy check and the whole second response. σ runs 0.20–0.34 m along this ridge, and nothing here could tell you which end you were on.

And the honest limit

None of this makes unreplicated designs wrong. 67 shots mapped a curved surface and ranked five factors. It makes them a different instrument — one that measures means and cannot measure how well. Knowing which you are holding is the job.

Lecture notes

Return to the three answers you wrote on the board during the title slide, when you asked how they would spend 67 shots and five knobs. Almost nobody proposes a sequence of three experiments with different purposes. Almost everybody proposes one big one. That gap is what the day was for.

The closing panel is the one to end on rather than the four numbered points. Students finish a DOE course believing the method is unboundedly powerful. It is not: it optimises the process you have. When the robust optimum still leaves you with σ = 0.21 m, the remaining variation is a property of the hardware, and the next move is engineering rather than statistics. Saying so is what separates a competent practitioner from someone running software.

Final question, if there is time: "You have 150 shots and the same problem on a new machine. How do you spend them?" A good answer allocates across screening, RSM and confirmation, and reserves some budget for the experiment they have not thought of yet.
